Absolute advantage: B produces more Y per hour; neither country has an absolute advantage in X (they tie).

Comparative advantage: A has the lower opportunity cost of X (2 Y versus 4 Y); B has the lower opportunity cost of Y (0.25 X versus 0.5 X).

Predicted trade: A exports X; B exports Y. For both to benefit from this direction of trade, the world relative price of X must lie strictly between 2 and 4 Y per X, assuming voluntary trade at the illustrated bundles.

Connecting to the PPFs: A's maximum output is 10 X or 20 Y. B's maximum is 10 X or 40 Y. The slopes are −2 and −4, respectively.

Run the experiment

Good X is exported by Country A

Country A can make 10 X or 20 Y (cost of X = 2 Y). Country B can make 10 X or 40 Y (cost of X = 4 Y). Thus A has comparative advantage in X; B in Y.

A receives Y in exchange. Exports are limited by A's production and B's ability to pay with Y.
Units of Y per X. Both countries can gain when 2 < price < 4, with suitable production and trade.

Static model: linear PPFs, two goods, no trade costs, balanced trade. The price is exogenous and not calculated from market clearing.

Where do the gains go?

The welfare proxy is each country's additional Y consumption compared with its own PPF at the same X consumption. Positive values mean consumption outside the PPF; negative values mean inside. This is not a complete measure of utility or national welfare.

What students can discover

  1. Start with full specialization. Move the world price from 2 to 4. Who captures more of the gains? Does total production change?
  2. Hold the price fixed and increase A's exports. Why does one country's consumption bundle change differently from the other's?
  3. Compare partial and full specialization. How does the size of the world production pie change?
  4. Move the world price below 2 or above 4. Can both countries still gain from this direction of trade?
  5. Does the largest total gain necessarily imply an equitable distribution of gains?
